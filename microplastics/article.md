# Introduction

Plastic. It’s everywhere: in vehicles, in bottles, in bags, even in our bodies. When plastics decompose over the course of 20 to 500, or maybe even over 1000 years, they break down into smaller particles called microplastics. These particles, defined as being less than five millimeters long, can permeate entire ecosystems, endangering everything they touch. As our world becomes increasingly reliant on plastics, these particles can pose serious health risks for animals and plants alike. In fact, in the last 80 or so years, humans have produced over eight billion tons of plastic. With only around 10% of it being recycled, there is a significant plastic waste and decomposition problem.

# Primary Microplastics

Primary microplastics are one of the two main types of microplastics. Primary microplastics are defined as microplastics that are engineered and designed on purpose. These microplastics are used in products like toothpaste, cosmetics, or facial cleaners. They are designed to complete a specific task, often acting like an abrasive to better clean something. For example, in the case of toothpaste, primary microplastics are used to grind away at plaque and to maintain the shelf life of the paste. The products they’re in aren’t meant to be consumed; most of the time, these products are washed down the sink. Like all microplastics, their small size allows a decent percentage of them to pass through most filtration systems, allowing them to spread around the environment, especially in waterways.  
When primary microplastics pass through waterways, they are often swallowed by small creatures. These creatures include small fish, plankton and shellfish. These animals will mistake the primary microplastics as food, interfering with feeding and nutrition. As larger marine animals eat these minute aquatic creatures, the microplastics move up the food chain, leading to the presence of microplastics in every level of the food cycle. Primary microplastics also act as magnets for harmful polymers and chemicals in the water which indirectly causes them to poison anything that eats them. As primary microplastics settle in water or on the ground, they can provide ideal breeding grounds for bacteria and even lead to the development of antibiotic resistant bacteria. Given that primary microplastics are so ubiquitous, humans often end up consuming them in relatively large amounts through food, water, or even air. These particles then spread throughout the body, causing cellular stress and damage. For example, studies have shown that microplastics in the intestines can lead to inflammation, oxidative stress (when reactive oxidants build up in the body, as a byproduct of cellular respiration, faster than the body can develop antioxidants to neutralize them), and changes in the microbial makeup of the gut. These direct effects are linked to autoimmune disorders, cancer, or even neurodegenerative diseases.

# Secondary Microplastics

The other type of microplastics are secondary microplastics. These are produced from the degradation of larger plastic objects like tires or water bottles due to weather, sunlight, and physical wear. Secondary microplastics are the most common type of microplastic, accounting for around 67-81% of all microplastics. They can contaminate soil and water ways, permeating through multiple food chains and ecosystems before being ingested by humans. Through their journey, they can also break down into nanoplastics, which are small enough to enter cells.

Similarly to primary microplastics, secondary microplastics attract chemicals and other polymers, meaning they can introduce the areas they travel through to new toxins. Physically, secondary microplastics have highly irregular shapes with large surface areas. This means that they enter and interact with ecosystems and absorb toxins significantly easier than primary microplastics. Regarding effects on plants, secondary microplastics can change the soil structure, making it harder for plants to grow and absorb nutrients. They can also reduce the water retention capabilities of soil and slow down the growth of plants.  
In waterways, secondary microplastics can slow down the growth of animals, provide false feelings of fullness, and increase the rate of ice and snow melting. When impacting humans, secondary microplastics have all of the same effects as primary microplastics with some additions. One of these additions is the increased accumulation of secondary microplastics in organs. Due to their larger average size, primary microplastics are often easier to flush out of the body. However, smaller secondary microplastics can stay and accumulate in organs, causing cellular damage and increasing toxicity. Secondary microplastics are also linked to an increased risk of chronic diseases affecting the cardiovascular, reproductive, and nervous systems.

# Nanoplastics

A separate category of microplastics are defined under the name of nanoplastics, microplastics that are defined as particles that are less than one micrometer (0.001 mm) in length. They are created through the decomposition of primary and secondary microplastics through physical (weather, UV rays, etc.), biological (bacteria secreting enzymes), or chemical (reactions with salt water, soils acids, pollutants, etc.) degradation. They can also be produced through specific industrial manufacturing. Nanoplastics are extremely ubiquitous, found in water, food, and air. In fact, in certain conditions, microwaving food in a plastic container has the potential to release a maximum of billions of these particles every time.

Since they are so tiny, nanoplastics have a more dangerous biological effect than larger microplastics. Their small size allows them to penetrate cells and reach the nuclei. When this happens, nanoplastics gain direct access to DNA, leading to irreversible cellular mutations and damage. The toxins and chemicals that the nanoplastics carry also get released directly into the nucleus of a cell, causing extreme damage. They can also breach biological barriers like the blood-brain barrier, a selective, semipermeable membrane used to separate the brain’s extracellular fluid. Nanoplastics can also get past human gut lining and lung tissue. Like the other two types of microplastics, nanoplastics attract toxins and chemicals, which can trigger various problems in the human body. Nanoplastics can also make it extremely difficult to get rid of bacteria biofilms, structured communities of microorganisms linked to each other and a surface. These bacteria biofilms can be found in places like dental plaque, infected wounds, slime in pipes, and industrial machinery. When nanoplastics enter a bacteria biofilm, they cause the bacteria to undergo oxidative stress. This causes the bacteria to release dormant viruses called prophages which multiply rapidly and burst out of the bacteria. Consequently, the other bacteria in the biofilm produce a sticky substance called Extracellular Polymeric Substances (EPS). This strengthens the shield around the biofilm, making it significantly harder to remove.  
Nanoplastics can also cause oxidative stress in multicellular organisms, something that can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction (when the organelles in a cell that produce the energy in a cell fail to produce an adequate amount of energy), DNA damage, or even cell death. Since nanoplastics are recognized as foreign objects in the body, they trigger an inflammatory response through the stimulation of proteins. Nanoplastics are also correlated with a significant increase in the risk of cardiovascular problems. Nanoplastics can accumulate in vascular tissues, increasing the risk of heart attack, strokes, and other heart problems. When nanoplastics cross the blood-brain barrier and enter the brain, they have the potential to cause neuroinflammation, something that can damage brain tissue. These nanoplastics can also cause behavioral changes and conditions that mimic Parkinson’s disease or dementia. Nanoplastics may also harm reproduction, because the toxic chemicals they attract damage sperm cells and ovarian follicles (the areas where eggs are developed and produced). If nanoplastics build up in the liver, they can disrupt key metabolic pathways for protein and glucose regulation.

# Actions

Clearly, microplastics of any kind are harmful to both the environment and humans. There are a few things that we can do as a society to alleviate this burden. The simplest option would be to recycle more. Currently, only around 10% of all plastic waste is recycled. However, by increasing this number, less plastic has the opportunity to break down, reducing the addition of microplastics into an environment. Additionally, companies can research biodegradable alternatives to plastic to replace the use of polymers that take forever to break down. One such alternative could be using a bag woven from bamboo. This would not only make the bag biodegradable, but it would also make the bag incredibly strong. Companies are also researching ways to take microplastics out from the environment. However, this technology is still extremely primitive and isn’t in a scalable place yet. One of the reasons for this is because microplastics are extremely small, bypassing a lot of filtration systems. Also, their polymer chain structures make it a lot harder to chemically attract them.

# Conclusion

Overall, there is a significant problem regarding plastic decomposition and microplastics. The two main types of microplastics, primary and secondary, as well as nanoplastics all have individual environmental and biological problems. Primary microplastics are pieces of plastic smaller than five mm that were created to serve a purpose, often found in facial cosmetics or toothpaste. Secondary microplastics are microplastics that were created through the degradation of a larger plastic object, accounting for around 75% of all microplastics in the world. Nanoplastics, microplastics less than one micrometer, have the ability to pass through biological barriers and into cells, wreaking havoc on the body. The problem of microplastics is especially relevant considering that the average human inhales or ingests around 100,000 pieces of plastic every year. Microplastics are associated with thousands of premature deaths worldwide. However, by recycling our plastic waste and researching synthetic and biodegradable alternatives, we as a community can work together to reverse this trend.
