## Introduction

Year after year, participants utilize light microscopes to capture the tiniest organisms they can find and submit their work to a contest called Small World in Motion. At Science by Sketch, we love all things that merge science and art. Microscopy is just that: it allows us to peer into a world that would have otherwise been invisible.

Nikon Small World began in 1975 as a competition for photography through light microscopes. In 2011, the Small World in Motion competition was introduced specifically for microscope videos and time-lapses, with each entry limited to 60 seconds.

All of the entries this year were fascinating, but there was one particular clip that caught our attention. 

## The Smallest Aquarium

Pleyer’s submission, \*Jellyfish Larvae Suspended in Water Droplets\*, placed third in the 2026 Nikon Small World in Motion competition. A microscopist and scientific imaging enthusiast, Pleyer captured the footage using darkfield microscopy at 25× magnification, revealing tiny jellyfish larvae suspended in individual drops of water.


But capturing the footage was not an easy task. Pleyer spent months experimenting with his setup, suspending individual drops of water from a thread above a Petri dish. Then, he carefully introduced the larvae into the droplets, turning each one into a miniature aquarium.

## What Are Jellyfish Larvae?

But what exactly are these tiny jellyfish? The organism in Pleyer’s video are jellyfish larvae, an early stage in a jellyfish’s life cycle. Before growing into the familiar jellyfish we see drifting through the ocean, they begin as microscopic free-swimming organisms. Their tiny size makes it hard to see them with the naked eye, which is part of what makes Pleyer’s footage so striking. 

## Seeing the Invisible

What makes the footage particularly interesting is that the water droplets were not simply a convenient way to contain the organisms. They were part of the artistic intent. Pleyer wanted viewers to understand just how small these jellyfish larvae really are by placing them inside something familiar: a single drop of water. Instead of simply telling us that these organisms are microscopic, the footage gives us a physical sense of their scale.

The months of effort required to capture a single, usable piece of footage makes the final result even more impressive.

Pleyer employed a technique called darkfield microscopy. Instead of illuminating the background, dark field microscopy causes the organisms to appear brightly. This makes them stand out, while they move through the background. Pleyer captured footage using a 25× microscope objective, turning a tiny drop of water into an entire world.
