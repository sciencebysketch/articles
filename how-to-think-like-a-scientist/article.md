If you didn’t catch that, the definition essentially explains that science is a way of learning about the phenomena and laws of our world. This can be done using the scientific method, which segues us into our next topic\!

## Overview of Scientific Method

Earlier, I mentioned thinking like a scientist requires an understanding of some ideas and concepts. The Scientific Method is one of them.

The Scientific Method is a process to prove or disprove a specific belief.

1. **Observe** \- What do you see that you haven’t before? Once you have caught a phenomenon in the wild, you can move on to the next step.

2. **Question** \- This one almost comes naturally after the first step: ask a question about what you saw. A question can just be asking _why_ something works; it can be really simple.

3. **Research** \- Does your question have a solution? If it’s a more complex question, the answer may not come as easily, and you’ll have to do research to understand what leading theories are or are not.

4. **Hypothesize** \- What do you think is the reason? A hypothesis **does not have to be true\!** These are what you think the cause(s) for the phenomena are/is, based on the research you’ve done. Now comes the fun part\!

5. **Experiment** \- Design a repeatable experiment that can test your hypothesis and can generate data. Experiments usually only test one variable or aspect that you think is related to your phenomenon. Run your experiment **multiple times** and collect the data.

6. **Analysis** \- What does the data say? What can you understand from this? Analysis also includes taking the **raw data** and putting it into an understandable form. Making graphs, tables, and other visuals can help you identify revealing patterns and help communicate your results. Make sure to note whether your hypothesis was proven\!

7. **Communicate** \- Spread the message (even with us)\! You can post this online to forums and even journal websites that expose your work to other scientists. A discovery won’t be recognized if the scientist(s) didn’t tell anybody\!

**One important note:** Neither you nor actual scientists have to follow this process exactly*.* These are guidelines to create a proposed explanation for your observation through unbiased, measurable tests. If you ran a fair experiment, one with a clear hypothesis and a well-made analysis, then you’re probably already following the Scientific Method\!

Here’s the part we’ve all been waiting for. _How do I actually make myself think like this daily?_

### 01\. Observe

Contrary to what the media may depict, your observations are one of the most important parts of the scientific method. The ability to look at the world and even recognize the phenomena present is what kicks off a great experiment and discovery\! However, I do acknowledge that this can also be the most difficult part of the process. For most, observational skills take time to properly develop. However, through constant reflection on your own life, you can better these skills and eventually apply them to conclude.

Here’s a good example: Take Isaac Newton’s understanding of gravity. He first hypothesized that the force that keeps us on the ground is the same that keeps the moon rotating around the Earth. Through some tests, he was able to expand his observation and claim that this force, gravity, is present in the entire universe. This massive breakthrough first came from his simple observation of gravity.

The observations and questions that you pose don’t have to be groundbreaking. They can be as simple as:

- “Why is grass green?”

- “Why is the sky blue?”

- “What’s the point of my pinky toe?”

Now, a quick Google search will most likely give you reasons for all of these **phenomena** you see. However, priming yourself to observe the world in this way will carry on into your adult life and benefit you no matter what path you take\!

### 02\. Make it hard for yourself.

That’s right, I said it. Scientists first have to make their _own_ observations and draw their _own_ conclusions before checking their work with a search engine or Artificial Intelligence. The order matters here too. If you expose yourself to what has already been discovered, you risk biases and coming to a conclusion you don’t understand. In other words, do the work yourself _before_ you compare with others. A good exercise: Take one observation you’ve made, and just _think_ about it. Try to come up with an explanation based on what you **already know.** This brings you closer, or even on the same playing field, as scientists researching new topics that have never been explored before. Learning these perceptive skills will not only help you ask the _why,_ but also generate the answer to your questions, even if you’re still a student.

Now, I’m not saying you shouldn’t ask for help _at all_. _Not_ asking for help will box you in and won’t help you get to a solution. Being able to ask for help is also another invaluable skill\!

### 03\. Be Curious

This will help you with trying to explain observations yourself (the second bullet point). Being curious is the true catalyst for all science. You can’t observe if you’re not curious about your surroundings.

Additionally, curiosity isn’t something someone is born with more than someone else.

Merriam-Webster defines the word curious as: “desire to know, and interest leading to inquiry.” The definition, in my opinion, is missing one defining characteristic of being curious: action. Although it is not technically a verb, the word "curious” comes from actively trying to gain knowledge and actively trying to observe or investigate. It’s a conscious effort that, like making observations, may take time to improve upon. But once you get better at being curious, generating questions like the bullet points above will come much more easily\!
